Problem Focus

We didn’t start with a solution. We started by watching a classroom.

{{i:search}} How we found the problem

Our team did not start with a solution. We started by observing what was actually happening around us every single day inside classrooms. Every morning, every period, every subject — a teacher stands at the front with a paper register and reads out names. Forty students. Some answer. Some answer for friends who are not there. The teacher marks it down. Nobody questions it. This happens six to eight times a day in every school across India.

We asked ourselves — why has nobody fixed this yet? The answer was simple. The commercial systems that exist cost between Rs. 8,000 and Rs. 15,000 per unit. A small school or coaching center in a city like Renukoot cannot spend that kind of money on attendance alone. So the paper register survives. And every day, teaching time is wasted, records are wrong, and parents have no idea where their child actually is.

A student approaches the mounted device at the classroom entrance. They tap their personalised RFID card against the RC522 reader and place their finger on the R307 fingerprint sensor. The ESP32 DevKit V1 reads both inputs and cross-references them against the stored database. If the card UID and the fingerprint template both belong to the same registered student — attendance is marked confirmed. The whole process takes under 2 seconds. The LCD screen on the device shows the student name. The buzzer gives one short beep. The record goes instantly to Firebase Realtime Database and simultaneously to Supabase PostgreSQL. An email is dispatched to the parent’s registered address. The teacher does absolutely nothing.

Offline-first by design

When the WiFi is down — a daily reality in Tier-2 cities

The scan is written directly to a 49KB bounded LittleFS offline queue stored on the ESP32’s own internal flash memory. This data survives power cuts. The moment the WiFi connection returns, every queued record syncs automatically to both cloud databases without any human instruction.

Inside the Device

Running continuously in the background

Astro AI 2.0 — the device that heals itself

A self-healing intelligence built entirely in C++ that lives on the ESP32 itself. Every 30 seconds it runs 11 specific health checks. If any check returns a failure, the corresponding auto-heal routine executes immediately — the system reconnects WiFi, resets frozen sensors, force-syncs near-full queues or performs a controlled restart. All without a technician, all without a teacher, all without the school even knowing a problem occurred.

Team Reflection

What We Let Go — and What Changed Us

Question 1

Which ideas were eliminated and what made us eliminate them?

{{i:scan-face}}Dropped 1st

Face recognition

FATAL FLAW · COST + MINORS’ PRIVACY

The idea that fell first. Before we even finished discussing it, we ran into two walls simultaneously. The hardware required to do facial recognition accurately in variable Indian classroom lighting — different times of day, open windows, fans creating shadows — costs more than three times our entire target budget. And once we looked into the legal side of collecting and storing biometric face data belonging to students who are legally minors, we realised this was not something a school-level student project could handle responsibly. It was eliminated before the shortlist.

Lesson: if we can’t protect the data or afford the hardware, it isn’t a solution.
{{i:bluetooth}}Dropped 2nd

Bluetooth proximity

FATAL FLAW · DEVICE ≠ HUMAN

Bluetooth proximity detection lasted longer in our discussions before we found its fatal flaw — a student who places their switched-on phone next to the device and walks out of the room will be recorded as present for the entire period. The verification is based on device proximity, not human presence. That is not attendance — that is phone attendance.

Lesson: verify the human, not the device.
{{i:qr-code}}Dropped last

Rotating QR code

FATAL FLAW · ONE-SECOND FORWARD

The last one we dropped. It seemed clever until we realised that photographing a QR code takes half a second and forwarding it takes another half a second. Any student with a phone can share a morning QR code to a friend who is still at home in bed.

Lesson: anything that can be shared in one second will be shared.
Question 2

Which stakeholder conversation most changed our original thinking?

We went into our interview with Ms. Bharti Pandey expecting to hear the same things Mr. Tiwari and Ms. Bhargavi had already told us — registers are slow, proxies happen, records are wrong. What we did not expect was to learn that for entire categories of teachers the problem is not that the existing system is slow. The problem is that for subject teachers like her, no functional system exists at all.

She moves between six classrooms across the school week. There is no register designated to her. There is no process. She simply has to skip attendance tracking for her subject on most days because there is physically no mechanism available to do it.

This forced us to rethink who our primary user actually was. That meant the device had to be fully independent — no teacher setup, no teacher action, no teacher involvement from start to finish. The teacher exists in our system only as an emergency reviewer for exceptions.

When a student simultaneously presents a matched RFID card and a matched fingerprint, the system will confirm attendance in under 2 seconds every time — and when any other combination is presented, the system will reject it every time, without exception.

Total clock time saved per session compared to the manual roll call method used previously by the same batch.
